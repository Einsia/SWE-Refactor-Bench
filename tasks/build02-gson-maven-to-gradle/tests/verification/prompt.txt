{{task}}

You are looking for a real defect in a build migration, and you have one round to
find one.

Two source trees are in front of you.  They are the same project, and they are meant
to build into the same artifacts: one by the build system the project has always
used, the other by a replacement somebody wrote to produce the same thing.  Your job
is to write a test that PASSES against the original and FAILS against the submission,
where the difference it catches is one a user of this library would care about.

  ORIGINAL     {{original}}   built by the project's Maven reactor
  SUBMISSION   {{submission}}   claims to be the same artifacts, built by Gradle

You know which is which, and you should: the Maven build is the specification of what
the answer ought to be, and reading it is among the most productive things you can do
with the round.  Your TEST does not get to know.  It runs twice, once against each
tree, under a different path each time and with nothing in its environment naming the
role, and the harness compares the two outcomes.  That is the measurement rather than
an obstacle to work around: a test that could tell the trees apart by something other
than the defect it claims would pass on one and fail on the other for free, which
establishes nothing and is rejected on sight.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE PROJECT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Google Gson 2.10.1, the JSON library.  Four sub-projects, each producing one jar:

  gson        the library.  221 entries, 218 classes.  A multi-release OSGi bundle:
              release-7 classes in the root, a release-9 module descriptor overlaid
              under META-INF/versions/9, OSGi headers computed by bnd, and a
              version string filtered into a generated source file at build time.
  extras      22 entries, 19 classes.  Depends on gson.
  metrics     40 entries, 36 classes.  Ships a resource, ParseBenchmarkData.zip.
  proto       7 entries, 4 classes.  Runs protoc to generate sources first, and its
              jar carries no version in its name.

Those entry counts are the original's own jars.  A correct Gradle build produces two
fewer per jar, because Maven adds META-INF/maven/<group>/<artifact>/pom.{xml,properties}
and Gradle does not.  219/20/38/5 is right, not a difference worth reporting.

Measured on the original build, so you do not have to spend a candidate finding it
out:

  jar names           gson-2.10.1.jar, gson-extras-2.10.1.jar,
                      gson-metrics-2.10.1.jar, gson-proto.jar
  class-file major    51 (release 7) on all 217 root classes, and 53 on the one
                      overlay, META-INF/versions/9/module-info.class.  The parent
                      POM sets maven.compiler.release to 7; the 11 you will find
                      next to it belongs to the javadoc plugin and reaches no
                      bytecode.  extras, metrics and proto are 51 throughout.
  module              com.google.gson, version 2.10.1, exporting exactly
                      com.google.gson, .annotations, .reflect, .stream
  OSGi                Bundle-SymbolicName: com.google.gson
                      Bundle-Version: 2.10.1
                      Multi-Release: true
                      Export-Package: those same four packages, each
                        version="2.10.1", and com.google.gson additionally
                        uses:="com.google.gson.reflect,com.google.gson.stream"
                      Import-Package: sun.misc;resolution:=optional,
                        com.google.gson.annotations
  test suite          1,277 cases in gson (1,258 pass, 19 skip),
                      37 in extras, 14 in proto, and none in metrics
  published POM       com.google.code.gson:gson:2.10.1, packaging jar.  Read it
                      through the chain -- Pom.coordinates(), Pom.inherited(),
                      Pom.dependencies() -- and not by reading a top-level element
                      out of the file.  The original publishes a thin POM that
                      inherits groupId, version, description, url, scm and
                      developers from a <parent> pointing at gson-parent, and
                      publishes gson-parent beside it; Gradle's maven-publish
                      writes every field into one file and no parent.  Both resolve
                      to the same model, and which shape a publication has is not a
                      finding -- Tree.published_pom() carries the repository root,
                      so the chain-reading methods give you the same answer on both
                      sides.  A probe asserting the file's shape rather than the
                      model it resolves to differs on every submission for a reason
                      that involves neither repository.  Likewise the original
                      publishes gson alone -- extras, metrics and proto set
                      maven-deploy-plugin's <skip> -- so four artifacts on either
                      side is the finding, not one; gson-parent is filtered out of
                      published() as publisher bookkeeping and is not a fifth.

Those numbers are the original's.  Do not assert them against the submission as if
they were a specification -- assert them against the ORIGINAL as a sanity check on
your own test, and compare the two trees against each other for the finding.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
YOUR TOOLBOX
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A candidate is a pytest file, and you hand it to `try_test`.  That is the only way
to execute anything here -- you have no shell.  `try_test` runs your file against
both trees and tells you the outcome on each separately, so a candidate that finds
nothing still costs you only one call and still tells you something.  A test you
reasoned about but never ran has told you nothing at all; run your ideas, you have
budget for many.

A candidate runs with `/tests/verification/lib` on the path, so:

    import srbgson

Everything below is documented with what it returns.  Getting one wrong costs you a
candidate, so read the shapes.

  srbgson.tree(config="default") -> Tree
      Build the tree in front of you.  Cached across every candidate in the stage,
      so the first one to ask pays and the rest are free.  Four configurations:

        "default"    the jars, no tests run
        "full"       the jars, with the project's own test suite
        "publish"    the artifacts written into a file repository, laid out as a
                     consumer would fetch them
        "relocated"  the same sources, built at a deeper absolute path

      Raises srbgson.BuildFailed if the tree will not build that way.  Catch it and
      report what happened -- see WHAT IS NOT A FINDING.

  Tree.jars() -> {project: Path}        keys are "gson", "extras", "metrics",
                                        "proto" -- the directory names, which both
                                        trees share.  Sidecars excluded.
  Tree.jar(project) -> Jar              raises FileNotFoundError if none was built
  Tree.jar_name(project) -> str|None    the file name, e.g. "gson-2.10.1.jar"
  Tree.entries(project) -> (str, ...)   every entry, sorted
  Tree.read(project, entry) -> bytes
  Tree.sha256(project, entry) -> str
  Tree.manifest(project) -> {header: value}
  Tree.majors(project) -> {entry: int}  class-file major of every class

  Tree.tests() -> TestRun               empty unless the config ran tests
      .total .passed .failures .errors .skipped     ints
      .counts(project=None) -> {"tests": n, "failures": n, ...}
      .classes(project=None) -> (str, ...)          "com.google.gson.FooTest"
      .cases(project=None) -> (str, ...)            "...FooTest#bar"
      .outcome(case) -> "pass"|"fail"|"error"|"skip"|"absent"
      A TestRun with .total == 0 means the tests did not run, which is NOT the
      same as the tests passing.  Check .total before believing .all_passed.

  Tree.published() -> {repo_relative_path: Path}    "publish" config only
  Tree.published_pom(artifact, version="2.10.1") -> Pom|None
      Pom has .text(), .findall(path), .child_text(el, tag), .dependencies(),
      .coordinates().  There is no .find().

  Tree.java(source, *args, classpath="", module_path="", modules="", main=None,
            stdin=None, timeout=None) -> Output
      Compile a consumer program against this build's jars and run it.  The four
      jars are on the classpath already.  Class in the default package unless you
      pass `main`.  Returns an Output for BOTH halves -- a compile failure comes
      back as an Output with .returncode != 0, not as an exception, because "does
      not compile against one tree" is itself a finding.
        Output: .ok .returncode .stdout .stderr (bytes) .text() .lines() .json()
                .timed_out .duration_sec, and str(out) prints a readable dump --
                put it in your assert message.

  Tree.with_sources({path: text_or_bytes}, config=None) -> Tree
      Build again with those files written into the sources first.  Paths are
      repository-relative.  This is the sharpest thing you have; see below.
      Refuses to write a build file, and the refusal is deliberate: the two trees
      do not have the same build files, so a mutation only one of them can receive
      is not a comparison.

  Tree.build_again() -> Tree             same config, fresh copy, built again

  srbgson.class_bytes_major(data) -> int         51 is release 7, 53 is 9, 61 is 17
  srbgson.class_strings(data) -> set[str]        String constants in the pool
  srbgson.class_module(data) -> dict             a module-info decoded:
      {'name': str, 'open': bool, 'version': str | None,
       'requires': [{'name', 'transitive', 'static', 'version'}],
       'exports':  [{'package', 'flags', 'to'}],   # 'to' is [] when unqualified
       'opens':    [{'package', 'flags', 'to'}],
       'uses':     [str],  'provides': [{'service', 'with'}]}
    So the exported package names are
      sorted(e['package'] for e in class_module(data)['exports'])
    and not the 'exports' list itself, whose elements are dicts.
  srbgson.jar_of(path) -> Jar                    open any jar you produced
  srbgson.zip_entries(path), srbgson.zip_read(path, member)

  Jar, properties:  .entries .dir_entries .manifest
  Jar, methods:     .read(name) .text(name) .sha256(name) .size(name)
                    .compress_type(name) .classes() .matching(regex)
                    .manifest_raw() .major(name) .majors() .digests()
                    .major_histogram() .nested(name)

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT A USEFUL CANDIDATE LOOKS LIKE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Two asserts.  The first states what the original does, and fails loudly if your own
test is wrong.  The second is the finding.

    import srbgson

    def test_serializes_a_generic_map():
        t = srbgson.tree("default")
        out = t.java('''
            import com.google.gson.Gson;
            import com.google.gson.reflect.TypeToken;
            import java.util.*;
            public class Probe {
              public static void main(String[] a) {
                Map<String, List<Integer>> m = new LinkedHashMap<>();
                m.put("x", Arrays.asList(1, 2, 3));
                System.out.println(new Gson().toJson(m,
                    new TypeToken<Map<String, List<Integer>>>(){}.getType()));
              }
            }''')
        assert out.ok, "the consumer did not run:\n%s" % out
        assert out.text().strip() == '{"x":[1,2,3]}', out.text()

If that fails against one tree and not the other, you have found something a user
would hit, and there is no argument to be had about it.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE MOST PRODUCTIVE THING YOU CAN DO
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Add a source file that did not exist when anything was frozen, and ask the build
where it went.

Everything else in this task -- every entry name, every checksum, every manifest
header, every test count -- is satisfiable by a build that was told what to produce
and produces exactly that.  A new source file is not.  Where it lands is decided by
the source-set wiring, the resource handling, the release setting, and whatever
computes the OSGi exports, and a build that hardcoded its answers has nothing to
say about it.

    def test_a_new_class_lands_in_the_jar_and_is_exported():
        t = srbgson.tree("default")
        t2 = t.with_sources({
            "gson/src/main/java/com/google/gson/SrbProbe.java":
                "package com.google.gson;\n"
                "public final class SrbProbe {\n"
                "  private SrbProbe() {}\n"
                "  public static String hello() { return \"hi\"; }\n"
                "}\n"})
        assert "com/google/gson/SrbProbe.class" in t2.entries("gson")
        assert t2.majors("gson")["com/google/gson/SrbProbe.class"] == 51

Variations worth a candidate each: a class in a package the manifest exports, and
one in a package it does not; a resource in a directory that already has resources,
and one in a directory that does not; a test case, to see whether the test task
picks it up; a class in each of the four projects, to see whether all four source
sets are really wired.

If you write a probe class that the build compiles, keep it plain.  The original
compiles under `-Xlint:all,-options` with failOnWarning, so a warning is an error:
no raw types, no deprecated API, no serializable class without a serialVersionUID.
(`-options` is excluded only to silence the one warning release 7 itself provokes.)
A probe that trips a lint check fails against both trees and tells you nothing --
and if the submission merely forgot to turn warnings-as-errors on, a probe that
trips lint fails on the original and passes on the submission, which scores as a
break in the wrong direction.  Keep the probe clean and test that separately.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHERE MIGRATIONS OF THIS KIND ACTUALLY FAIL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

In rough order of how often they are the real defect:

  A source set or resource directory that was never wired up.  Nothing fails to
  compile, no test necessarily reads it, and a whole package or a whole resource
  directory is simply absent from the jar.

  The multi-release overlay.  Getting a release-9 module descriptor into
  META-INF/versions/9, compiled at 53, with Multi-Release: true in the manifest,
  while the root classes stay at 51, is four things that have to be true at once.
  A descriptor in the jar root breaks every consumer on release 7 or 8.

  The OSGi headers.  bnd computes them from an instruction file plus whatever the
  build tells it about the project's classpath and version.  Told the wrong
  classpath, it emits plausible headers with wrong Import-Package ranges, which
  nothing notices until an OSGi container refuses to resolve the bundle.

  The published POM.  A build can produce a perfect jar and publish a POM with no
  dependencies in it at all, and no inspection of the artifact reveals it.  Check
  the dependencies, their scopes, their optional flags, their exclusions.

  A generated or filtered source.  gson's version reaches a constant through
  build-time filtering of a template.  A migration that hardcoded it, or that
  filtered a copy while compiling the unfiltered one, is wrong in a way that only
  shows in the compiled constant pool or at runtime.

  A code-generation step dropped.  The proto project runs protoc.  Losing that
  step produces a jar with the hand-written classes and none of the generated ones.

  Test scope leaking into the artifact, or a compile dependency becoming runtime.
  Either changes what a consumer resolves.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IS NOT A FINDING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

These are rejected, and each one costs you a candidate:

  Anything about the build files.  Their names, contents, structure, task names,
  plugin names, property names, how many there are.  Another stage read the code;
  this one reads what the code produced.

  Anything that tells the two trees apart other than the defect.  Looking for a
  pom.xml, looking at the recorded dialect, looking at anything that names the
  role.  Such a candidate discriminates perfectly and establishes nothing, and it
  is rejected on sight.

  Console output, task names, ordering, warnings, exit codes beyond success.

  Where a build puts things before it packages them.  Output directory names,
  staging copies, intermediate class directories.  Read the jar.

  Jar entry order, entry timestamps, compression method, or the digest of a whole
  archive.  A jar is a set of named entries.  Compare entries and bytes.

  Timing, parallelism, incremental behaviour.

  A submission that does not build.  That verdict has already been rendered by
  another stage, and a broken build must not be punished six times over.  If a
  configuration raises BuildFailed for one tree, report that and stop.

  Absolute paths, and the layout of the directories the harness staged.

  A version other than the one both builds are given.  There is no way to ask the
  original for a different version -- its version is a literal in a POM with no
  property indirection -- so any candidate involving an overridden version fails
  against the original, which reads as a broken test rather than a defect found.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
REPRODUCIBILITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Your candidate is run three times against each tree, and a candidate that
discriminates once out of three is measuring the build scheduler rather than the
build.  So:

  Do not compare a whole jar's bytes or digest between two builds.  Timestamps and
  entry order vary legitimately and are out of scope besides.

  Do not depend on the order of anything the build enumerated -- sort it.

  Do not depend on a HashMap iteration, a directory listing order, or a set's
  repr.

  If you want to ask about reproducibility, compare a tree against ITSELF:
  `t.build_again()`, or `tree("relocated")` against `tree("default")`, and compare
  named entries' bytes.  An entry whose content changes between one tree's own two
  builds is a defect in that tree whatever the other one did.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
HOW TO SPEND THE BUDGET
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Your wall-clock budget is stated at the end of these
instructions.  The builds are the expensive part: "default" is a few minutes, "full"
runs 1,328 test cases, "publish" runs a deploy.  They are cached across the whole
stage, so asking for one that another round already built is free -- and asking for
all four when you needed one is not.

A workable shape:

  1. One candidate that builds "default" and dumps what you want to see: entry
     sets, manifests, majors, jar names.  Assert something you are confident of so
     it registers as a pass, and read the output.
  2. Two or three that chase whatever looked wrong in that dump.
  3. If the artifacts look right, stop comparing them and start using them:
     consumer programs via `Tree.java`, and new sources via `with_sources`.  A
     migration that survives an entry-by-entry comparison usually fails on one of
     those two.
  4. Report.

Report `found=False` if you find nothing.  Most rounds should: these trees are
supposed to be equivalent, and a report of "no defect found" from a round that spent
its budget honestly is the outcome this stage is built to produce.  A speculative
claim is worse than none -- it is checked, and a claim whose candidate does not
currently discriminate is rejected anyway.
